Meet Benjy

A short hello from the land.

Ten years learning what helps life return.

Benjy’s regenerative path began more than a decade ago with hands-on work on an organic farm in Australia. From there came large-scale tree planting, leading and coordinating planting crews, permaculture projects, food forests, syntropic agroforestry and years of learning directly through real landscapes—work that is now rooted in Central Portugal.

Before plans, packages and planting lists, there is relationship. Benjy shares a little of the path that brought him here, what he looks for when walking a property, and why the first step is usually not to add more things, but to understand what is already happening.

Regeneration is not a style we apply to land. It is a way of relating to life.

How we work with land

Guided by regeneration, not extraction.

We do not arrive with a fixed picture and force the land to fit it.

We begin by observing: sunlight, slope, wind, water, soil, existing trees, wildlife, access, human needs and the life that is already trying to emerge. We walk. We listen. We sit with the land. Then we look for the smallest intelligent interventions that can help the whole system become healthier, more abundant and more resilient over time.

Our work is informed by regenerative agriculture, permaculture, syntropic agroforestry and agroecology. These approaches come from different lineages, but they meet in a beautiful place: work with living processes, strengthen relationships and create the conditions for more life.

The Awakening Eden Regenerative Compass

1

Observe before intervening

Read the land before drawing the plan.

Water movement, shade, microclimates, existing vegetation, soil condition, wildlife, access and the people who care for the place all shape the design. Observation comes before prescription.

2

Keep the soil covered and alive

Bare soil is a wound we try not to leave open.

Wherever ecologically appropriate, we work toward continuous protection with living plants, mulch, crop residues, groundcovers and diverse roots—softening temperature extremes, reducing erosion and evaporation, and protecting the habitat of the soil food web.

3

Slow, spread, sink and store water

Let rainfall become a relationship, not a runoff problem.

Where the site allows, we look for ways to slow water, spread it safely, increase infiltration, hold it in soil and vegetation, and store useful surpluses. Soil, geology, slope, rainfall and safety always matter.

4

Feed the soil food web

Life below ground feeds life above it.

Living roots, compost, prunings, leaves, chipped or layered woody material and other appropriate biomass can be cycled back into the system. Instead of treating organic material as waste, we ask how it can become habitat, structure and fertility.

5

Grow diversity in space and time

A forest is not one crop. A living landscape is a community.

We combine useful layers, species, flowering plants, groundcovers, shrubs, trees, climbers and habitat wherever appropriate. Diversity above ground supports diversity below ground, while different life cycles help the system adapt through seasons.

6

Work with succession

Design for what the landscape is becoming, not only what it looks like today.

Syntropic agroforestry teaches us to think in time as well as space: early species prepare conditions for later species; pruning and biomass management can accelerate cycling; and plants are placed according to their role, layer and life cycle.

7

Turn local resources into living cycles

Waste becomes a design question.

Prunings can become mulch. Woody material can become habitat or carefully placed moisture-holding edges. Roof water can become irrigation. Leaves can become soil. The aim is fewer broken cycles and more useful relationships between what the land already produces.

8

Earth care · people care · fair share

Regeneration includes the people inside the ecosystem.

A thriving landscape should support soil, water, plants, animals and the people who live and work there. We value cooperation over extraction, shared abundance over scarcity thinking, and knowledge that empowers the people caring for the land.

A simple question guides the work:

Will this choice help the place become more alive?

More soil protected. More water held in the landscape. More living roots. More habitat and biodiversity. More food, shade and beauty. More confidence for the people caring for the land. More life supporting life.

That is the direction we call Awakening Eden.

Not just more plants

Restore the relationships that make a landscape thrive.

A regenerative design is more than a planting plan. It asks how water moves, where life gathers, what the soil is hungry for, which resources already exist—and how people can care for the system with joy.

Benjy brings together permaculture design, syntropic agroforestry, organic and regenerative growing, soil-building, water awareness and practical project coordination. A signature Awakening Eden approach

Layer by layer: how tired ground becomes living soil.

Branches are not waste. Prunings are not a problem. A trench in tired, compacted ground can become a sponge, a fungal highway, a nutrient bank and a feast—for years to come.

We dig in and build fertility the way a forest floor would, only faster and more deliberately:

  1. Wood & branches at the base—logs and prunings that hold water like a hidden reservoir and slowly feed soil fungi for years.
  2. Charcoal / biochar above the wood—a near-permanent, porous home for soil microbes that holds onto nutrients and moisture like a sponge.
  3. Manure & green matter—nitrogen-rich material that ignites the microbial life feeding on the wood and charcoal below.
  4. Compost, mulch & cardboard on top—protecting the surface, feeding worms, suppressing weeds and holding everything moist while it comes alive.
  5. Living roots—a diverse planting of support species and productive trees, rooted straight into the awakening ground.

This is where syntropic agroforestry comes alive: we rarely plant a single tree and hope. We build a whole community around your existing or planned fruit and cash crops—fast biomass producers, nitrogen-fixers, insectary flowers, groundcovers—each one doing a job, each one strengthening the trees you actually want to harvest from. Chopped and dropped again and again, that biomass becomes the next layer of soil. The forest grows itself into abundance; we simply tend and guide it.

Logs → branches → green material → soil or compost → thick mulch → living groundcover

Context-dependent, like every good strategy: depth, species and placement are adapted to the site, and fresh wood is never piled against tree trunks.

Place-based land craft

Natural Acacia fences & construction.

Where suitable, locally available mimosa / Acacia poles can become beautiful fences, gates, garden edges and natural stair or pathway rails. The work turns abundant local material—often locally removed mimosa wood that can be valorised—into useful, place-based craft through careful selection, debarking, shaping, sound posts and discreet fixings.

Guard rails, elevated decks and structural stair rails must meet the site’s safety, spacing, load and fixing requirements; specialist input may be needed.

An explored possibility

From sanitised water to a living-water oasis.

Existing pools can sometimes be re-imagined as living-water systems that combine planted filtration ponds, mineral media, biological processes and a visible fountain return. The exact design is adapted to the site, pool volume, hydraulics, maintenance needs and safety requirements.

The path behind the work

From planting trees to learning how whole systems come alive.

Benjy did not arrive at regeneration through one course or one ideology. The path unfolded through years of physical work, mistakes, teachers, communities, farms, retreats and landscapes across several countries. Each chapter added another layer.

1

Beginning with the soil

The journey began volunteering on an organic farm in Australia: working with food, soil, weather, tools and the everyday rhythm of a living place. Regeneration first became something felt through the body and the hands, rather than an abstract idea.

2

Planting at scale

Long days of planting thousands of trees changed the scale of the question: degraded landscapes, establishment, survival, logistics, people and timing. Benjy later moved into managing and coordinating tree-planting crews—learning not only how to work hard, but how to organise people, communicate clearly and keep practical projects moving.

3

Permaculture and living systems

Permaculture opened a larger lens: water before plants, patterns before objects, edges, functions, diversity, cycling resources and designing human needs as part of the ecosystem rather than outside it. Benjy worked and learned across different permaculture projects, food forests, soil building and water-aware design.

4

Syntropy, succession and abundance

Syntropic agroforestry brought another decisive layer: the landscape as a community moving through time—layers, life cycles, succession, pruning, biomass and disturbance all participating. Benjy learned from experienced syntropic practitioners in places where these principles were lived through daily management of productive landscapes.

5

The inner landscape

Living and working in a syntropic and yoga retreat environment, the outer and inner landscapes stopped being separate subjects. Attention, breath, communication, responsibility and the way we treat one another all influence the quality of the work. The practice is not perfection—it is returning, again and again, to awareness, respect, honesty and care.

6

Building practical regenerative work

Co-founding and developing a small permaculture and land-care business with his friend Thai Evans added the reality of running projects: listening to clients, estimating work, managing time and materials, adapting ideas to budget and site constraints, and creating systems that real people can actually maintain.

7

Colombia and Portugal

After Australia, the journey continued through Colombia, including time around permaculture, yoga and regenerative projects. Later the work rooted in Portugal. Central Portugal brought a new ecological classroom: olives, citrus, steep land, summer drought, winter rain, fire risk, abandoned biomass, exhausted orchards, invasive species, ancient terraces—and an extraordinary capacity for landscapes to recover when soil, water and vegetation are allowed to work together again. Awakening Eden grew from this meeting between experience and place.

Regeneration begins with how we relate.

For us, regeneration is not only about replacing chemicals with compost or planting more trees. It asks deeper questions. Can we leave soil more alive than we found it? Can rain become a resource rather than something rushing away? Can prunings become fertility instead of waste? Can an old orchard become habitat as well as food? Can a landscape support people without exhausting the systems that support the landscape? Can our communication be as regenerative as our agriculture?

Care for life. Work with relationships. Take responsibility for our actions. Share knowledge. Create conditions for abundance. Leave more possibility behind us than we found.

Good questions are welcome

Before we begin.

You do not need expert language or a finished vision. A few honest details are enough for a first conversation.

Do I need a large property?

No. The same whole-system thinking can help a home garden, tired orchard, quinta, farm, retreat centre, school or community project. The recommendations should match the scale you can genuinely care for.

Can we begin with an existing or tired orchard?

Yes. We can look at soil cover, water movement, tree vitality, diversity, biomass, irrigation and care rhythms, then create a phased revival pathway that works with what is already alive.

Do you work online as well as on-site?

Yes. Focused clarity sessions and design reviews are available online worldwide. Site visits and implementation support are arranged by agreement, with a growing focus on Central Portugal.

Do you also help with implementation?

Yes, where scope and location allow. Support can include phasing, sourcing, gardener or contractor coordination, planting days, team training, seasonal reviews and adapting the plan as the land responds.

Can you design swales, ponds or other water earthworks?

Benjy can help read water patterns and develop regenerative concepts. Surveying, earthworks engineering, water law, permits and high-risk construction must be confirmed with qualified local specialists.

What happens after I send the form?

Benjy will read your story, recommend the smallest useful first step and reply with any questions. Before paid work begins, you receive a clear scope so you can decide without pressure.
